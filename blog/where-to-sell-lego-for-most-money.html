Where to Sell Your LEGO for the Most Money

A channel-by-channel look at where LEGO actually sells for the most, with typical fees, effort levels, and how to price correctly before you list.

If you have a shelf of retired sets or a bin of loose bricks, the question is rarely whether LEGO holds value, but where to sell LEGO to keep the most of that value in your pocket. The same set can net wildly different amounts depending on the channel, its fees, and how much work you are willing to put in.

There is no single best marketplace. The right answer depends on what you are selling, how fast you want the money, and whether you are moving sealed investment sets, complete used sets, or bulk brick by the pound. Below, we compare the main channels on fees, effort, and typical payout, then cover the step most sellers skip: pricing correctly so you never undersell.

Price Before You List

The biggest mistake is guessing a price from active listings. Active asking prices are wishful; sold prices are reality. Before choosing a channel, look up what your exact set (by number, in your condition) has actually sold for recently. A good pricing tool is BrickGains, which aggregates real sold data across marketplaces so you can set a realistic floor and ceiling before you commit to a platform.

Grounding your price in sold history does two things: it stops you leaving money on the table on a set that quietly appreciated, and it stops you overpricing a common set that will never move. Once you know the number, the channel decision becomes a trade-off between fees and effort rather than a guess.

How to Price With Sold Data

Sold-data pricing is a repeatable process, not a hunch. Start by filtering to the last few months so seasonal spikes and one-off outliers do not skew your read. Discard the highest and lowest sales; the honest market value usually sits in the middle of the recent range. Then adjust for your specific condition: a sealed set with a crushed corner sells below a pristine one, and a used set missing a single rare minifigure can drop noticeably in value.

Separate the two questions a listing must answer. First, what is the item worth in your condition? Second, what does each channel cost to reach a buyer? A set that sold for a consistent range across marketplaces gives you a firm anchor; checking aggregated sold prices gives you that anchor quickly, and from there you subtract fees to compare your true net per channel. Setting your asking price slightly above the recent median leaves room to accept reasonable offers while staying grounded in what buyers have actually paid.

BrickLink: Best for Sets and Loose Parts

BrickLink is the specialist marketplace built for LEGO, and it reaches buyers who know exactly what they want. It is unmatched for selling by the part and strong for complete sets, because pricing is transparent and the audience is knowledgeable. Builders come to BrickLink hunting specific elements to finish a MOC or replace a lost piece, which means individual parts that would be worthless in a bulk lot can command real money here.

Seller fees are modest, typically in the low single-digit percent range, plus payment processing. The catch is effort: parting out a set means accurate inventorying, and buyers expect precise condition grading. For collectors and part-outs, the higher net usually justifies the labor. Cross-check your listing against recent sold comps using a valuation tool so you match the market instead of undercutting it.

eBay: The Widest Audience

eBay reaches the largest pool of casual and collector buyers, which makes it excellent for sealed sets, minifigures, and anything with broad demand. Auctions can surface true market value for rare items, while fixed-price listings suit predictable sets. Its built-in sold-listings filter is also one of the most useful free pricing references available, so even if you sell elsewhere it is worth checking eBay's completed sales.

The trade-off is cost. Final-value fees generally land in the low-to-mid teens percent once payment processing is included, and international shipping adds complexity. eBay tends to win when reach and speed matter more than squeezing out every last point of margin. Promoted listings can raise visibility on a crowded platform, but the extra ad fee eats further into your net, so use them only when a set is genuinely competing against many identical listings.

Facebook Marketplace and Groups: Low Fees, Local Cash

Facebook Marketplace and dedicated LEGO buy/sell groups can carry the lowest fees of any channel, sometimes none for local cash pickup. That makes them attractive for bulk lots and heavy sets where shipping would eat your profit.

The downsides are a smaller, less specialized audience and more haggling. Local sales avoid shipping risk entirely, but you will meet strangers and field lowball offers. For big, awkward items sold nearby, the fee savings often outweigh the friction. Meet in a public place, take cash rather than unfamiliar payment apps, and confirm the exact set and condition in writing before you agree, so a buyer cannot claim a piece was missing after the handoff.

Fees Compared: What Each Channel Really Costs

Fees are the quiet difference between channels selling the same set, and they compound with shipping. BrickLink sits at the low end, with seller fees in the low single-digit percent range plus payment processing, which is why it nets the most on parts and complete sets despite its extra labor. eBay lands in the low-to-mid teens percent once final-value and payment fees are combined, the price you pay for its reach. Facebook is effectively free for local cash but offers no buyer protection and the smallest audience.

The rule of thumb is simple: as fees fall, effort and risk usually rise. A near-zero-fee local sale demands your time, a meetup, and negotiation, while a higher-fee platform buys you reach and convenience. Run the same set through each channel on paper, subtract fees and shipping, and compare the real net rather than the headline price. Frequently the channel that looks cheapest is not the one that leaves the most in your pocket once your hours are counted.

Bulk Buyers and Local Options

If you want speed over maximum value, bulk buyers, brick-and-mortar resellers, and consignment shops will take everything at once. You will typically receive a fraction of retail value, but you offload a large collection in a single transaction with no listing work.

This route makes sense for mixed, incomplete, or low-value bulk where the per-item effort of selling individually simply is not worth it. Get more than one offer, and sanity-check it against a rough sold-value estimate so you know what you are trading away for convenience.

Bulk vs Individual: Which Nets More?

Selling individually almost always yields a higher total, but it costs you time and often shipping supplies. Parting out a set on BrickLink or listing minifigures separately can multiply the return versus dumping everything as one lot, because you capture the buyers who want one specific thing. The catch is that dozens of small listings mean dozens of packages, messages, and trips to the post office.

Selling in bulk trades money for speed. If your time is scarce or the collection is mostly common bricks, a single bulk sale is the rational choice. A practical middle path is to cherry-pick the high-value items, sealed sets, rare minifigures, and sought-after parts, to sell individually, then move the leftover common bricks as one bulk lot. That way you capture most of the upside without drowning in low-value listings.

Shipping Without Killing Your Margin

Shipping is where profit quietly disappears, especially on heavy sets. Weigh and measure before you list so you can either bake the cost into your price or set an accurate shipping charge; a surprise postage bill can turn a healthy sale into a break-even one. Reuse clean boxes and packing material, and compare carrier rates rather than defaulting to one, since dimensional weight can make a large light box cost more than you expect.

Pack to survive the journey. Sealed sets need rigid protection so corners and seals arrive intact, because box condition is a major part of their value. For used and bulk sales, bag parts securely and cushion them so nothing rattles loose. Good packing also protects you from returns and negative feedback, which cost far more than an extra sheet of bubble wrap.

Avoiding Scams and Chargebacks

Higher payouts attract bad actors, so protect yourself on every channel. On shipped sales, use tracked, insured postage and photograph the item and packaging before it leaves, so you have evidence if a buyer falsely claims damage or an empty box. Be wary of buyers who push you to move off-platform or pay by unusual methods; that is a common setup for a chargeback with no recourse.

For local deals, meet in public, count cash on the spot, and treat any offer far above asking as a red flag rather than good luck. Documenting condition honestly in your listing is also self-defense: a clear, accurate description with flaw photos gives a marketplace little reason to side with a buyer disputing a problem you already disclosed.

Condition and Photos Move the Needle

Whatever channel you choose, presentation drives your final price. Describe condition honestly: note whether a box is creased, whether instructions and all minifigures are present, and whether a set is complete. Buyers pay a premium for certainty and punish surprises with returns.

Use clear, well-lit photos from multiple angles, including any flaws. For sealed sets, show the seals; for used sets, show the actual parts, not a stock image. Strong photos and accurate descriptions consistently earn more than the same item listed carelessly, regardless of platform.

Frequently asked

Where can I sell LEGO for the most money?

It depends on what you are selling. BrickLink usually nets the most for loose parts and complete sets thanks to low fees and a specialist audience, while eBay reaches the widest pool for sealed and rare items. Facebook Marketplace has the lowest fees for bulk and local sales. Always price against real sold data first so you pick the channel that maximizes your net.

How do I know what my LEGO is actually worth?

Look up your exact set number in its condition and check recently sold prices, not active asking prices. Active listings show what sellers hope to get; sold prices show what buyers actually paid. Aggregating sold data with a valuation tool gives you a realistic price floor and ceiling before you list.

Is it better to sell LEGO sealed or opened?

Sealed, retired sets in good box condition typically command the highest prices, especially for sought-after themes. Opened but complete sets still sell well if all parts, minifigures, and instructions are present. Incomplete or heavily used sets are usually better sold as bulk or parted out on BrickLink.

Do selling fees really make a big difference?

Yes. Fees range from near zero on local Facebook sales to the low-to-mid teens percent on eBay once payment processing is included. On a high-value set, that spread can mean tens of dollars. Factor the channel's fees into your expected net before deciding where to list.

Should I sell my LEGO in bulk or piece by piece?

Selling individually usually earns more in total but takes far more time and packaging, since each part or set becomes its own listing and shipment. Selling in bulk is faster but pays a fraction of retail. A good compromise is to sell high-value sealed sets, rare minifigures, and sought-after parts individually, then move the remaining common bricks as a single bulk lot.
